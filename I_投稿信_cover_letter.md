**Re: Original Article submission — "Remifentanil and hyperalgesia reporting in two national pharmacovigilance databases: a head-to-head disproportionality study with negative controls defined a priori"**

I am submitting the above manuscript for consideration as an Original Article in *Anaesthesia*. It is a single-author, methodologically oriented pharmacovigilance study on a topic that your readership discusses continually in the context of remifentanil infusions: opioid-induced hyperalgesia.

**What the study does and what it found.** The clinical literature on remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia is contested, but no one has looked at what real-world reporting actually contains. I performed a head-to-head disproportionality analysis of remifentanil against fentanyl, sufentanil and morphine in two independent national spontaneous reporting databases — the United States Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (accessed through openFDA, 20 692 687 reports) as the primary analysis, and the Health Canada Canada Vigilance line-listing (1 154 017 reports) as an independent confirmation set. Four non-paradoxical opioid side effects served as negative controls defined a priori, and a non-pain term served as a specificity probe.

Two results stand out. First, the word used in clinical practice for the syndrome, hyperalgesia, is not a preferred term in either coding dictionary: a search on it returns nothing, in either database. Every outcome term was therefore verified as retrievable before any zero was read. The preferred term that actually carries the concept is present in both corpora, and once it is queried the signal appears for all four opioids, remifentanil included — although remifentanil's is the weakest of the four, rests on ten reports, and did not reproduce in the smaller Canadian database. Second, and independently of that terminology problem, remifentanil's head-to-head ratios were below one for pain in both databases and for all four negative controls against both comparators in the larger database, in the same direction across serious-report restriction and across ten calendar years. A specificity probe behaved inconsistently between the two databases, which argues against a uniform reporting artefact.

**Why I think this belongs in *Anaesthesia*.** The message is neither "remifentanil is safe" nor "remifentanil causes hyperalgesia." It is that in this data source the answer depends on the preferred term chosen, and that the term clinicians use is not the term the dictionary stores. That has two consequences your readers and reviewers will care about: the absence of a reporting signal must not be read as reassurance, and the stream of null results this data source will keep producing on hyperalgesia, tolerance and withdrawal should be reported as a terminology problem rather than as accumulating evidence of safety. The study is reported in accordance with the READUS-PV recommendations, and the completed checklist is supplied as Supporting Information.

**Statements.** This work is original, has not been published previously and is not under consideration elsewhere. As sole author I conceived the study, designed and performed the analysis, interpreted the data, wrote the manuscript, and take full responsibility for its content and for the correct referencing of all supporting work. The analysis was not prospectively registered; the term groups, the negative controls and the specificity probe were defined a priori and documented in the Methods in a dated analytical plan archived with the analysis code. Both datasets are publicly available and de-identified, so no ethics approval or informed consent was required. There is no funding to declare and I have no competing interests.

**Data and code availability.** The analysis code and every derived result file are permanently available at <https://github.com/yyx-4113/remifentanil-hyperalgesia-signal-faers-canada>, and neither raw source dataset is redistributed. The manuscript's number-to-source traceability table lets a reviewer follow any reported figure back to the file that produced it, and the archived repository includes an automated consistency gate that re-checks every number quoted in the manuscript against those files.

**Use of generative AI — confirming the disclosure.** In accordance with the Journal's policy and the Association of Anaesthetists' position statement on artificial intelligence, the manuscript carries a full disclosure under *Acknowledgements*. To restate it here: large language model assistants, accessed through a desktop AI agent environment, were used to draft and debug the analysis scripts, to write the plotting code, to edit the language of the text, and to help retrieve references and check their bibliographic records against Crossref, PubMed and the publisher record, with every candidate citation that did not resolve to a real record discarded and all 30 cited references verified by identifier. No reported data or result was created, generated, imputed, altered or manipulated by generative AI, and no AI tool was used to create, alter or manipulate the figures.
