**Re: Original Article submission — "Remifentanil and hyperalgesia reporting in two national pharmacovigilance databases: a head-to-head disproportionality study with prespecified controls"**

I am submitting the above manuscript for consideration as an Original Article in *Anaesthesia*. It is a single-author, methodologically oriented pharmacovigilance study on a topic that your readership discusses continually in the context of remifentanil infusions: opioid-induced hyperalgesia.

**What the study does and what it found.** The clinical literature on remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia is contested, but no one has looked at what real-world reporting actually contains. I performed a head-to-head disproportionality analysis of remifentanil against fentanyl, sufentanil and morphine in two independent national spontaneous reporting databases — the United States Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (accessed through openFDA, 20 692 687 reports) as the primary analysis, and the Health Canada Canada Vigilance line-listing (1 154 017 reports) as an independent confirmation set. Four non-paradoxical opioid side effects served as prespecified negative controls, and a non-pain term served as a specificity probe.

Five of seven prespecified hyperalgesia terms returned zero reports in both databases. The single analysable hyperalgesia-adjacent term showed no remifentanil signal while fentanyl and morphine showed strong signals, and every computable head-to-head ratio for pain and for all four negative controls was below one, in the same direction across serious-report restriction and across ten calendar years. The specificity probe reversed direction, which argues against a uniform reporting artefact.

**Why I think this belongs in *Anaesthesia*.** The message is not "remifentanil is safe." It is that spontaneous reporting is structurally incapable of detecting opioid-induced hyperalgesia, because the syndrome has no preferred term that encodes a reportable event. That has two consequences your readers and reviewers will care about: the absence of a reporting signal must not be read as reassurance, and the stream of null results that this data source will keep producing on hyperalgesia, tolerance and withdrawal should be reported as a structural limitation of the instrument rather than as accumulating evidence of safety. The study is reported in accordance with the READUS-PV recommendations, and the completed checklist is supplied as Supporting Information.
