**Re: Original Article submission — "Remifentanil and hyperalgesia reporting in two national pharmacovigilance databases: an observational head-to-head disproportionality analysis"**

I am submitting the above manuscript for consideration as an Original Article in *Anaesthesia*. It is a single-author, methodologically oriented pharmacovigilance study on a topic that your readership discusses continually in the context of remifentanil infusions: opioid-induced hyperalgesia.

**What the study does and what it found.** The clinical literature on remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia is contested, but no one has looked at what real-world reporting actually contains. I performed a head-to-head disproportionality analysis of remifentanil against fentanyl, sufentanil and morphine in two independent national spontaneous reporting databases — the United States Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (accessed through openFDA, 20 692 687 reports) as the primary analysis, and the Health Canada Canada Vigilance line-listing (1 154 017 reports) as an independent confirmation set. Four established, non-paradoxical opioid effects served as comparator terms — they are not negative controls in the causal sense, since a ratio below one shows that the drug is reported less, not that it causes less — and an unrelated term served as a specificity probe.

Two results stand out. First, the word used in clinical practice for the syndrome, hyperalgesia, is not a preferred term in either coding dictionary: a search on it returns nothing, in either database. Every outcome term was therefore verified as retrievable before any zero was read. The preferred term that actually carries the concept is present in both corpora, and once it is queried it meets the signal criterion for all four opioids — but the ten remifentanil reports behind it turn out to be separate safety report identifiers for two patients, nine of them for one man, and the Canadian database, which de-duplicates at source, recorded none. It is reported as a term-level demonstration, not a signal. Second, and independently of that terminology problem, remifentanil's head-to-head ratios were below one for pain in both databases and for the comparator terms against both comparators in the larger database, in the same direction across serious-report restriction and across the eight years in which an estimate was possible. A specificity probe behaved inconsistently between the two databases, which argues against a uniform reporting artefact.

**Why I think this belongs in *Anaesthesia*.** The message is neither "remifentanil is safe" nor "remifentanil causes hyperalgesia." It is that in this data source the answer depends on the preferred term chosen, and that the term clinicians use is not the term the dictionary stores. That has two consequences your readers and reviewers will care about: the absence of a reporting signal must not be read as reassurance, and the stream of null results this data source will keep producing on hyperalgesia, tolerance and withdrawal should be reported as a terminology problem rather than as accumulating evidence of safety. The study is reported in accordance with the READUS-PV recommendations, and the completed checklist is supplied as Supporting Information.

**Statements.** This work is original, has not been published previously and is not under consideration elsewhere. As sole author I conceived the study, designed and performed the analysis, interpreted the data, wrote the manuscript, and take full responsibility for its content and for the correct referencing of all supporting work. The analysis was not prospectively registered; the term groups, the comparator terms and the specificity probe were specified in a dated analytical plan and documented in the Methods in a dated analytical plan archived with the analysis code. Both datasets are publicly available and de-identified, so no ethics approval or informed consent was required. There is no funding to declare and I have no competing interests.

**Data and code availability.** The analysis code and every derived result file are available at the study repository under the MIT licence, release `v1.6.0` (`https://github.com/yyx-4113/remifentanil-hyperalgesia-signal-faers-canada/releases/tag/v1.6.0`); the commit history and the earlier releases remain on `main`. Neither raw source dataset is redistributed. Every reported figure is traceable to the file that produced it, and the archived repository includes an automated consistency gate that re-checks each number quoted in the manuscript against those files.
